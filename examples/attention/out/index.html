The idea

What the paper proposes, in plain words, and why it matters.

Attention lets each query look at every key and decide how much of each value to copy. The query and key vectors are compared with a dot product; dividing by √dk keeps those scores from growing with dimension, and a softmax turns the scores into weights that sum to one. The output for each query is then the weighted average of the value rows.

Why it mattersThis single equation is the core of the transformer: it is how every token gathers information from every other token, and the 1/√dk factor is what keeps that gathering trainable at large dimensions.

Notation

Symbols used on this page

Every symbol, written the way the paper writes it.

SymbolMeaning
Qmatrix of queries, one row per query, each of dimension dk
Kmatrix of keys, one row per key, each of dimension dk
Vmatrix of values, one row per key, each of dimension dv
dkdimension of the queries and keys (number of columns of Q and K)
dvdimension of the values (number of columns of V)
QKTmatrix of raw dot-product scores, entry (i, j) = qi · kj
softmaxapplied to each row of scores, turning them into non-negative weights that sum to 1
Mechanism

How it works

The calculation, one step at a time.

  1. Form the raw scores S = QKT: each entry is the dot product of one query with one key.
  2. Divide every score by √dk (the scaling toggle switches this off).
  3. Apply softmax to each row of scores to get attention weights that sum to 1.
  4. Multiply the weights by V: each output row is the weighted sum of the rows of V.

    Try it yourself

    Guided explorations

    Two short experiments: what to change, what to watch, and why it happens.

    Exploration 1Equal scores give a uniform average

    Change
    Set Q and K so every query–key dot product is the same, then compare the weights with and without scaling.
    Observe
    Every weight becomes 1/n, so each output row is the plain average of the rows of V — and this is unchanged by the scaling toggle.
    Why
    Softmax of a constant vector is uniform regardless of the constant, so dividing by √dk cannot change equal scores.

    Exploration 2One dominant score sharpens the weights

    Change
    Make one key align strongly with the query and turn scaling off, then turn it back on.
    Observe
    Without scaling the softmax saturates: the top weight jumps toward 1 and the output collapses onto that value row; with scaling the weights stay softer.
    Why
    The raw dot product grows with dk, and a large score makes softmax put almost all mass on the largest entry; dividing by √dk shrinks the gap between scores.

    Keep in mind

    A limitation to remember

    Where this explanation stops being true.

    The demo uses tiny hand-entered matrices, so it shows the mechanism, not the paper's trained models or its experiments; the excerpt's claim that scaling matters more at large dk is only visible here as a trend, not as a measured result.

    What comes from the paper, and what is our own example.

    Attention Is All You Need, §3.2.1, Eq. (1)

    https://arxiv.org/html/1706.03762v7

    From the paper
    Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax(QKT / √dk) V
    “We compute the dot products of the query with all keys, divide each by √dk, and apply a softmax function to obtain the weights on the values.”
    “Dot-product attention is identical to our algorithm, except for the scaling factor of 1/√dk.”
    “To counteract this effect, we scale the dot products by 1/√dk.”
    Our example / simplification
    • Q, K and V are small editable matrices with toy integer entries, not learned projections.
    • The scaling toggle replaces the paper's fixed 1/√dk with either 1/√dk or 1, so the learner can see the difference.
    • No masking, multi-head splitting, dropout or positional encoding is included; only the single-head equation is shown.
    • The 'large dk' effect is illustrated by the size of the entries the learner types, not by retraining a model.

    This is a small illustrative demo; it does not reproduce the paper's experimental results.
